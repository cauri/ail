## 1. Purpose

This guide is for interviews with business and engineering leadership: CTOs, VPs of Engineering, Engineering Directors, and business executives (CEO, COO, CPO) who are responsible for AI adoption strategy and investment decisions.

The expert interviews (see [Expert Interview Guide](/research/expert-interview-guide)) validate the *technical accuracy* of the framework. The practitioner interviews (see [Practitioner Interview Guide](/research/practitioner-interview-guide)) validate the *recognizability* of the zone descriptions from the ground level. These leadership interviews validate the *organizational investment and goal-setting dimensions* of the framework -- the parts that are written for the people who have to fund and authorize the work.

Specifically, these interviews are testing:
- Whether the organizational investments the framework recommends are the right ones and in the right order
- Whether the framework's framing of zone selection as a strategic choice matches how leaders actually think about AI investment
- Whether the described benefits are the ones that actually motivate leadership decisions
- Whether the framework surfaces risks and obstacles that leaders recognize and care about

---

## 2. Leadership Recruiting Criteria

Appropriate participants meet at least two of the following:

- Currently serving as CTO, VP Engineering, VP Product, Engineering Director, or equivalent in an organization with an active software production function
- Holds or held budget authority for AI tooling, training, or infrastructure in a software production context
- Has made at least one significant AI adoption decision in the past 18 months (a decision with meaningful budget, headcount, or policy implications -- not just approving tool licenses)
- Is actively planning or evaluating AI adoption investments
- Serves as CEO, COO, or CPO and is involved in AI adoption as a strategic business decision, not a purely technical one

**Target composition:** Primarily engineering leaders (CTO, VP/Director Engineering), with 2-3 business-level leaders (CEO/COO/CPO) to capture the non-technical investment perspective. Aim for a mix of organization sizes: at least one startup or scale-up, at least one mid-size organization (200-1000 engineers), at least one enterprise.

**Exclude:** Leaders whose AI exposure is purely observational (board members who haven't been operationally involved); leaders who have not yet begun any AI adoption (they can't validate investment descriptions from experience).

## 3. Interview Introduction

Use this script to open the interview. The framing here is different from the practitioner interview: leaders need to understand they are evaluating the *investment and strategy* dimensions, not being assessed on their organization's current state.

---

*"Thanks for your time. Let me explain what we're working on and what I need from you.*

*We're developing a framework to help organizations make deliberate decisions about AI adoption across all the crafts involved in software production -- engineering, product, design, QA, and others. The framework describes different levels of AI adoption -- from individual practitioner usage through team-level integration through more advanced transformation -- and for each level, it identifies what organizational investments are needed and what benefits organizations can expect.*

*We've built this from practice experience, but we need to pressure-test it against the people who actually make these investment decisions. That's why I wanted to talk to you specifically. I'm not interested in where your organization is on this scale -- that's not what this conversation is about. I'm interested in whether the framework's description of what it takes to progress, and what you get when you do, matches how you actually think about these decisions.*

*I'll be specific: I want to know if the investment descriptions are realistic, if the benefit descriptions are compelling to you and your stakeholders, and if the framework misses anything important about how these decisions actually work at the executive level.*

*With your permission, I'll record this so I can focus on the conversation. It's for internal research only and any quotes will be attributed anonymously."*

---

## 4. Strategic Decision Questions

**1. What's your organization's current approach to AI adoption across software production -- is there a deliberate strategy, or has it been more organic?**

Probes:
- Who drives AI adoption in your organization -- leadership, individual practitioners, a dedicated platform team?
- Is there a budget specifically allocated for AI tooling and adoption, or does it come out of general engineering or product budget?
- How do you currently evaluate whether your AI investments are producing value?
- What originally prompted your attention to AI adoption -- competitive pressure, practitioner advocacy, board directive, something else?

**2. How do you think about AI adoption as a strategic investment rather than a tooling decision? What makes it different from deciding to adopt a new cloud provider or development methodology?**

Probes:
- Is AI adoption primarily a cost decision, a capability decision, a competitive decision, or a talent decision in your organization?
- Who else has a stake in this decision -- is it engineering-driven, or do you have significant input from product, finance, or the board?
- What's your planning horizon for AI investments -- are you thinking in quarters, years, or longer?

---

## 5. Investment Pattern Questions

**3. The framework identifies specific organizational investments required at each stage of AI adoption. I'm going to walk through the Zone 1 and Zone 2 investments. For each one, I'd like to know: is this the right investment? Is it realistic for your organization? And is there anything critical that's missing?**

*Walk through the investments for Zone 1 (individual-level AI adoption) and Zone 2 (team-level integration). For each investment, ask:*

Probes:
- Have you already made this investment, or is it ahead of where you are?
- If you haven't made it, what's blocking it -- budget, policy, organizational will, or something else?
- Would this be a hard sell to your own leadership (CFO, board, CEO)? Why?
- How long would this realistically take to implement in your organization?

**4. Looking back at AI investments you've made -- what worked better than expected, and what was a disappointment?**

Probes:
- What made the successful investments work? Was it the investment itself, or the conditions around it?
- What would you do differently if you were starting over?
- Were there investments you made that you now think were unnecessary? Were there investments you wish you'd made sooner?

**5. Are there organizational investments that the framework doesn't mention that you believe are critical for AI adoption to succeed?**

Probes:
- Changes to hiring, performance evaluation, or engineering career ladders?
- Organizational structure changes -- team composition, reporting lines, creation of new roles?
- Legal, compliance, or security investments specific to your industry?
- Cultural changes the framework describes inadequately or ignores entirely?

---

## 6. Zone Selection Questions

**6. The framework describes all four zones as a single linear progression where organizations choose how far to go based on strategic context. Zone 2 is described as the typical near-term target, with Zones 3 and 4 requiring progressively larger investments. Does this framing match how you think about AI investment depth?**

Probes:
- Is there a point where more AI adoption feels like "too much" for your organization?
- How do you balance the risk of over-investing versus under-investing in AI?
- Would you challenge the premise that Zone 2 is the right minimum target for most organizations? Why or why not?
- What would motivate you to pursue Zone 3 -- where AI starts to drive development rather than assist it?

**7. The framework identifies "zone targeting factors" -- strategic and operational conditions that suggest an organization should aim for a particular zone rather than the adjacent one. Do the factors described for your current or target zone match the way you actually think about how deep to go?**

Probes:
- What factors most influence your decision about how aggressively to invest in AI adoption?
- Does the framework's framing capture those factors, or does it miss the real drivers?
- Are there factors that matter enormously to you that the framework doesn't surface?

---

## 7. Measurement Questions

**8. How do you currently measure whether AI adoption is working? What data do you actually look at?**

Probes:
- Which metrics do you trust, and which do you suspect are misleading?
- What would a clear signal of AI adoption success look like for your organization -- what would you need to see?
- Are your current measurement approaches showing you anything useful, or is AI impact still essentially unmeasurable in your context?

**9. The framework defines expected benefits for each zone. For the benefits described at your current or target zone, which ones would be most compelling to your stakeholders when you're making the case for AI investment?**

Probes:
- Which benefits can you actually measure with your current instrumentation?
- Which would require new measurement capability to verify?
- Are there benefits that matter deeply to you personally but don't land with your stakeholders?
- Are there benefits missing from the framework that would be important to your organization?
- The ACE diagnostic is a facilitated self-assessment -- team members rate their own behavior on a frequency scale, and results are discussed in a facilitated workshop. How credible is that to you as a measurement approach? What would make you more or less confident in the results?

---

## 8. Risk Questions

**10. What risks concern you most about AI adoption -- the things that keep you cautious or slow you down even when the opportunity looks compelling?**

Probes:
- Is the biggest risk technical (AI-generated code quality, security vulnerabilities), organizational (skill gaps, cultural resistance, team fragmentation), or strategic (competitive parity, vendor lock-in, regulatory exposure)?
- How does your organization currently manage those risks? Is your current approach adequate?
- Does the framework surface these risks in a useful way, or does it underweight them?

**11. How does the framework address -- or fail to address -- the organizational risks you care most about?**

Probes:
- Are there risks the framework ignores entirely?
- Are there risks the framework surfaces that you wouldn't have thought to ask about -- things that turned out to be real?
- Is there guidance you'd want from a framework on risk management that's absent here?

---

## Closing

*"Last question: if a framework like this told you exactly what organizational investments to make and in what order to make them, how credible would that be to you? What would it need to show or prove to earn your trust as a decision-making tool?*

*[Let them answer.]*

*Anything we didn't cover about how you think about AI investment decisions?*

*[Let them respond.]*

*Thank you. This is exactly the kind of input that helps us build something that's useful to people actually making these decisions, not just people advising from the outside."*

## Interviewer Notes

**Focus on feasibility and motivation.** The primary question for leadership interviews is not "is this accurate?" but "would this work in your organization, and would it motivate you to act?" Pay close attention to where leaders express budget skepticism, political obstacles, or doubts about whether investments are feasible.

**Watch for the make-vs.-buy distinction.** Leaders often distinguish between investments they can make through internal will (policy changes, cultural expectations) and investments that require budget approval from above. Note which framework investments fall into each category for each participant.

**Listen for what's not said.** If a leader enthusiastically endorses all the investment descriptions without qualification, probe harder. Either the framework is describing investments they've already made and found genuinely effective, or they're being collegially agreeable. "Which of these would be a hard sell to your CFO?" often surfaces more honest assessment than "are these the right investments?"

**Note the decision-making process.** Leaders will often reveal how AI investment decisions actually get made in their organization -- who has to approve, what evidence they need, what objections tend to block progress. These process descriptions are valuable data for understanding the organizational investment context.

**Avoid positioning the framework as the solution.** The goal of this interview is validation, not sales. If a leader seems like a potential client, that's for a different conversation. In this interview, you need their honest critique more than their enthusiasm.
