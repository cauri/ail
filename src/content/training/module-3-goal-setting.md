## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, trainees will be able to:

1. **Present diagnostic findings to leadership in a way that informs strategic decision-making.** Translate zone and stage assessments from the team-facing diagnostic into business-framed investment recommendations that leadership can act on.

2. **Explain the progressive competency model and zone selection factors to non-technical leaders.** Communicate how all four zones form a single progression where organizations choose their stopping point, in language that does not trigger either panic or unjustified ambition.

3. **Facilitate a goal-setting session that produces committed target zone declarations, not aspirational wishes.** Run the session structure that moves leadership from "here are the findings" to "here is what we will invest in and who owns it."

4. **Recognize when target zone declarations are not grounded in real investment commitment.** Identify the signals -- vague sponsorship, absent budget authority, zone selection driven by prestige -- that indicate a declaration is aspirational rather than strategic.

5. **Document target zone decisions with rationale in a way that creates accountability.** Produce a zone selection record that captures not just the decision but the reasoning, the specific investments, and the named owners.

## Content Outline

### Session 1: From Diagnostic to Goal-Setting

**The transition between phases.** The diagnostic tells the organization where it is. The goal-setting session determines where it should go. These are different conversations requiring different participants, different framing, and different facilitation skills. The diagnostic involves the team. The goal-setting session involves leadership. The diagnostic surfaces behavioral evidence. The goal-setting session surfaces strategic intent and investment commitment.

The facilitator's role in the transition: bridge the two conversations. Leadership has received the management report. The goal-setting session is not the moment to re-explain the diagnostic methodology -- it is the moment to use what the diagnostic revealed as the foundation for a strategic decision.

**What leadership needs to hear: the strategic picture, not the technical details.** The management report contains the detailed findings. The goal-setting session should not re-present those details. Leadership needs to understand three things: where the organization is relative to competitive baseline (Zones 1-2), what strategic choices are available to them (Zones 2-3-4), and what those choices cost. Technical details about Plan/Code/Verify workflows or AGENTS.md configuration are not relevant to this conversation -- systemic investment gaps and competitive positioning are.

**Presenting the management report effectively.** The facilitator walks leadership through the management report before the zone-selection discussion begins. This is not a reading exercise -- it is an orientation. Move through findings quickly, flag the two or three most strategically significant patterns, and explicitly name what is and is not a question for leadership. "The team's Zone 2 scores indicate Practicing-stage adoption, with one structural gap: mandatory feedback loops are not enforced by the CI/CD pipeline. Closing that gap requires organizational infrastructure investment, which is a leadership decision. That is what we are here to discuss today."

### Session 2: The Progressive Competency Model in Practice

**Explaining Zones 1-2 as competitive baseline without triggering panic.** The message that every organization needs to reach Zone 2 can land in two unhelpful ways: as an accusation ("you're behind") or as an emergency ("you need to fix this immediately"). Neither is the goal. The right framing: "Zone 2 is where effective software development teams are heading across the industry. It is not exotic -- it is becoming the expectation. The diagnostic tells us how close your teams are to that expectation, and what specific investments would close the gap."

Avoid urgency language that triggers defensive reactions. Leadership who feel accused of organizational failure tend to dispute the findings rather than engage with the investment case.

**Explaining later zones as progressively larger investments.** Two failure modes: leadership picks Zone 3 or 4 because it sounds impressive (ambition without strategic grounding), or leadership dismisses later zones as unnecessary because Zone 2 sounds adequate (complacency without competitive analysis). The right framing presents each zone transition as a decision with genuine trade-offs: "Each step along the progression requires a larger investment. Zone 3 requires structural change and sustained investment. For some organizations in some competitive contexts, those costs are justified by the throughput and capability gains. For others, they are not. The decision framework we will work through today is designed to answer that question for your specific situation."

**The common failure: leadership picks the highest zone because it sounds impressive.** This is the most frequent mistake in goal-setting sessions. A CEO who has read about "AI-first development" or heard a competitor announce an AI strategy arrives at the goal-setting session ready to declare Zone 4. The facilitator's job is not to simply record that declaration -- it is to test whether it is grounded in strategic analysis. "Zone 4 is an interesting target. Let me show you what it requires and we can work through whether that investment is justified for your organization." The decision framework (Session 3) is the tool for this conversation.

**The other common failure: leadership picks Zone 1 because Zone 2 sounds expensive.** The inverse failure mode: leadership hears "shared configuration, mandatory feedback loops, infrastructure investment" and retreats to "let's just make sure everyone is using AI tools." Zone 1 without Zone 2 investment is a predictable dead end. Individual AI adoption without team-level systematization produces uneven, unsustainable results. The facilitator must be direct about this: "Zone 1 without Zone 2 is where most AI adoption initiatives stall. The organizational cost of Zone 2 investment is real, but the organizational cost of stopping at Zone 1 is also real."

### Session 3: Zone Selection Decision Framework

**The four decision factors.** Risk appetite, investment capacity, strategic need, and competitive position. The facilitator must be competent in all four and in the questions that surface each factor in a leadership conversation.

**Risk appetite** measures how much structural disruption the organization can absorb. Risk increases along the progression. Zone 0->1 involves additive changes. Zone 1->2 involves process and infrastructure changes. Zone 2->3 involves structural reorganization. Zone 3->4 involves cultural transformation. Each transition adds a new dimension of risk: additive -> process -> structural -> cultural. Organizations with low risk tolerance should not target Zone 3 regardless of strategic ambition. The facilitation question: "What is the largest organizational change your engineering org has absorbed in the past two years, and how did it go?"

**Investment capacity** measures whether the organization has budget, time, and management attention to sustain the transition. Zone transitions are not one-time purchases -- they are 6-24 month sustained investments. An organization that cannot commit sustained resources will stall partway through and revert. The facilitation question: "Can you name specific budget and protected engineering time that is already allocated to this initiative? Not aspirational budget -- allocated budget."

**Strategic need** measures whether the target zone serves competitive position. Not every organization benefits equally from AI-accelerated development. A company whose advantage comes from regulatory expertise or customer relationships may find Zone 2 delivers everything it needs. A company competing on shipping velocity in a crowded product market may find Zone 3 is a strategic necessity. The facilitation question: "If your teams shipped at twice the current velocity, what would you do differently? Is that a competitive game-changer, or would it just reduce backlog?"

**Competitive position** measures what the industry is doing and what happens if the organization does not invest. The facilitation question: "Which of your competitors are visibly investing in AI-augmented development? What are you seeing them do that you are not doing?"

**When to push back on target zone selection.** Push back when: leadership is selecting a zone that the diagnostic does not support as a realistic near-term target (Zone 3 target when the team is at Zone 1 Learning); when the selection is aspirational with no named budget owner; when Zone 4 is selected without acknowledging the maturity-of-practice considerations; or when Zone 3 is selected but leadership has said they are not willing to change role definitions or create an AI Engineer role. The push-back is not confrontational -- it is clarifying: "I want to make sure we are setting a target you can actually achieve. To reach Zone 3, we would need [specific structural changes]. Are those on the table?"

**The investment commitment test.** A target zone declaration that cannot pass this test is not a commitment -- it is a wish. The test: "Can you name the specific investments you will make, assign a person who owns each one, and give me a realistic timeline?" If the answer is "we'll figure that out in the roadmap," the goal-setting session is not finished. Investment commitment is part of zone selection, not a downstream step.

### Session 4: Facilitating the Goal-Setting Session

**Who must be in the room.** Budget authority and organizational authority. The goal-setting session does not work with participants who cannot commit investments. If the VP of Engineering does not have budget authority, the CFO or a budget-authorized executive must participate. If the session includes people who can analyze but not decide, the session will produce analysis, not commitment. Clarify attendees with the sponsor before scheduling.

**The session structure.** Present findings (20-30 minutes): walk through the management report highlights, emphasizing systemic patterns and investment gaps rather than scores. Discuss systemic patterns (20-30 minutes): explore the organizational factors behind the diagnostic findings -- what structural conditions produced these results, and which conditions leadership can change. Review the decision framework (30-45 minutes): work through the four decision factors together. Declare target zones (15-20 minutes): leadership articulates their target zone. The facilitator tests the declaration for grounding using the investment commitment test. Commit investments (20-30 minutes): for each investment required by the target zone, name a specific investment, a responsible owner, and a timeline. Close with next steps (10 minutes): agree on the roadmap design session and the zone-selection document review timeline.

**Handling competing priorities.** Leadership teams in goal-setting sessions often surface other priorities mid-session: "We have a major platform migration happening. Can AI adoption wait?" The facilitator's job is not to prevent this conversation -- it is to keep it productive. "That is important context. Let's think about how it affects the decision. Does the platform migration make AI adoption harder to invest in right now, or is there a way to integrate these initiatives?" The goal-setting session should end with clarity about the decision -- including a decision to delay -- not with an unresolved priority conflict.

**Handling political tensions.** In some organizations, different leadership stakeholders have different stakes in the AI adoption agenda. The CTO wants to invest; the CFO wants to contain costs; the VP of Engineering is skeptical. The facilitator should be aware of these tensions from the discovery phase and should not pretend they are not present. Name the tension explicitly when it emerges: "I'm hearing two different perspectives on the investment case. Let me try to map out the trade-off explicitly so we can discuss the specific disagreement."

**Handling defensive reactions.** Leadership who feel that the diagnostic findings reflect poorly on their organization will sometimes dispute the findings rather than engage with the investment case. The facilitation response: redirect from the assessment to the decision. "Whether the current Zone 2 score is Practicing or Competent is less important than this question: what investment, if any, are you prepared to make? Let's start there and work backward."

### Session 5: Documenting the Decision

**What the Target Zone Declaration must contain.** A declaration that contains only a target zone is not a declaration -- it is an intention. The Target Zone Declaration must contain: the current zone and stage as established by the diagnostic; the target zone and the strategic rationale for that selection; the specific investments leadership has committed to, with named owners and timelines; the declared timeline for the transition; and any conditions or reservations noted during the session.

**The difference between a target zone declaration and an aspiration.** An aspiration sounds like: "We want to reach Zone 3 in the next 12 months." A declaration sounds like: "We are targeting Zone 2 Mastery by Q4. Specific investments: [CFO name] will approve the CI/CD infrastructure budget of $X by [date]; [VP Engineering name] will protect 15% of sprint capacity for AI practice improvement through Q3; [CTO name] will update role descriptions to include AI workflow requirements by [date]." The test is specificity. If the document could have been written before the goal-setting session without anyone in the room, it is an aspiration.

**Creating accountability: who owns each investment, by when.** The zone-selection document serves as the foundation for the roadmap (Module 4) and as the reference point when implementation encounters friction. When, six months later, the CI/CD infrastructure budget has not been approved, the facilitator can return to the document: "We agreed that [name] would approve this budget by [date]. What happened?" Without this document, every subsequent conversation about stalled progress starts from zero. With it, there is a shared baseline.

### Session 6: Organizational Investment Planning

**Moving from target zones to investment commitments.** Zone selection produces a destination. Investment planning produces the commitments that make the destination reachable. Each zone has a known set of required investments documented in the investment catalog. The facilitator uses these as a starting-point checklist during the investment commitment phase of the goal-setting session, not as a comprehensive list -- every organization's investment map will have organization-specific items.

**The cross-team investment view.** Many investments required for zone transitions benefit multiple teams simultaneously: CI/CD infrastructure changes, tool licensing decisions, policy updates, training programs, and role definition changes. These cross-team investments are the specific items that belong in the management report and the goal-setting session -- they are what distinguishes organizational investment from team-level adoption.

When multiple teams have been through the diagnostic, the goal-setting session should identify which investments serve all teams and allocate them at the organizational level rather than asking each team to invest independently. A shared AGENTS.md starting template, a standardized tool license structure, and a shared feedback loop infrastructure are all more efficient as organizational investments than as seventeen separate team-level decisions.

**Sequencing investments: what must come first.** Not all investments can happen simultaneously. Some are prerequisites for others: tool licenses before workflow training; CI/CD feedback loops before mandatory quality gates; role definition updates before AI Engineer hiring. The facilitator helps leadership identify the critical path: "Of these five investments, which is the dependency that blocks everything else? That is the one to sequence first."

## Learning Activities

### Activity 1: Zone Selection Simulation

**Format:** Small groups, 45 minutes

Each small group (3-4 trainees) receives a detailed organizational profile: a mid-size product company in a competitive SaaS market, currently at Zone 1 Learning, with moderate budget capacity, a CTO who wants Zone 3, and a diagnostic that shows the team has sporadic AI tool usage and no shared Zone 2 practices. The profile includes the four decision factors as observed through discovery interviews.

Groups work through the decision framework, assess each factor, and produce: a target zone recommendation with rationale; the top three investments required for the transition; and the specific push-back they would offer the CTO's Zone 3 preference.

Groups present their recommendations. The facilitator guides discussion around disagreements, particularly around whether the Zone 1 Learning starting point changes the near-term target even if Zone 3 is the ultimate aspiration.

### Activity 2: Responding to "We Want Zone 4"

**Format:** Role-play pairs, 30 minutes

One trainee plays the facilitator. One plays a CEO who has just read a competitor's press release about an "AI-first engineering organization" and arrives at the goal-setting session declaring Zone 4 as the target. The CEO persona card includes: no allocated budget beyond tool licenses, a track record of abandoning initiatives at 18 months, and a team currently at Zone 1 Practicing with no Zone 2 practices.

The facilitating trainee must respond to the Zone 4 declaration without dismissing it, use the decision framework to surface the gaps between the declaration and the organizational reality, and guide the CEO toward a grounded target zone declaration. After 15 minutes, pairs switch roles with the scenario reversed: the facilitator must respond to a VP of Engineering who wants to stop at Zone 1 to avoid "organizational disruption." After both rounds, the group debriefs: what techniques worked for redirecting without confronting?

### Activity 3: Investment Commitment Exercise

**Format:** Full group, 30 minutes

The group receives a target zone declaration for a Zone 2 target: a five-bullet list of intended investments that contains no named owners, no specific budget figures, and no timelines. Working individually, trainees identify which elements of the declaration are investments and which are aspirations, then draft the specific follow-up questions they would ask during a goal-setting session to convert each aspiration into a commitment.

The group then works together to produce a revised version of the declaration that passes the investment commitment test. The facilitator highlights which questions produced the most accountability-clarifying information and which questions leadership is likely to resist.

### Activity 4: Full Goal-Setting Session Role-Play

**Format:** Facilitated simulation, 60-75 minutes (capstone activity)

This activity simulates the complete leadership goal-setting session arc, not just individual facilitation moves. It is the highest-stakes exercise in the module.

**Setup:** Three trainees play leadership roles using detailed persona cards. The remaining trainees observe and take structured notes. Roles rotate so everyone facilitates at least one round.

**Persona cards:**

- **CTO (Persona A):** Technical. Has read the management report carefully. Wants Zone 3 because she is genuinely worried about falling behind competitors. Asks pointed questions about the diagnostic validity. Has budget authority but is not the final decision-maker on organizational structure changes. Will commit to Zone 3 if the investment case holds up.

- **VP of Engineering (Persona B):** Skeptical. Zone 2 makes sense to him. Zone 3 sounds like a reorg that will disrupt delivery at a critical time. He has seen initiative fatigue in this organization. He will raise this concern directly and will push back on timeline estimates. His team's buy-in matters to him; he will not commit over their heads.

- **CFO (Persona C):** Not technical but financially rigorous. Wants to understand what "investment" means in concrete budget terms. Will ask about ROI and about what "not doing this" costs the organization. Has final budget authority. Will not approve open-ended budget commitments; she needs specificity.

**The scenario:** The management report shows 5 teams at Zone 2 Practicing. The diagnosis indicates that Zone 2 is genuine but fragile -- two of the five teams have shown signs of regression under delivery pressure. One team has strong Zone 3 signals in how they are experimenting with AI-driven workflows. The competitive context (a product company in a growth market) suggests Zone 3 might be justified, but the organizational capacity evidence from discovery is mixed.

**The facilitating trainee must:**
1. Present the management report findings in under 10 minutes (not re-reading -- orienting)
2. Run the decision framework discussion using the four factors (risk appetite, investment capacity, strategic need, competitive position)
3. Handle the CTO's enthusiasm, the VP's skepticism, and the CFO's budget rigor simultaneously
4. Close the session with a grounded zone target, named investments, and owners -- not aspirations

**Debrief:** After each facilitation round, observers provide structured feedback: What did the facilitator do when the CTO and VP disagreed? How did they handle the CFO's ROI question? Did the session produce a real commitment or an aspiration at the end? What would they do differently?

## Assessment

### Case Study Assessment

**Format:** Take-home

Trainees receive a 2-page diagnostic scenario including: a management report summary for a 50-person engineering organization in the financial services sector, currently at Zone 2 Learning, with a VP of Engineering who wants Zone 3 and a CFO who wants to contain costs. The scenario includes discovery interview notes and the four decision factors as observed.

Trainees produce:

1. **Goal-setting session plan** (1 page): the session agenda, the key facilitation moves, and the specific decision framework questions they would use for this organization's leadership context.

2. **Draft Target Zone Declaration** (1 page): the target zone they would recommend, the strategic rationale, the specific investments they would require leadership to name and own, and the timeline framing.

Assessment evaluates whether the trainee's zone recommendation is appropriate to the organizational context, whether the declaration passes the investment commitment test, and whether the session plan reflects a realistic facilitation approach for this specific leadership dynamic.

## Preparation for Module 4

After completing Module 3, trainees should:

- Review the zone reference documents for the specific organizational investments required at each zone transition, with attention to sequencing and dependencies.
- Review the roadmap templates and consider: given a target zone declaration with named investments, what does a realistic phased progression plan look like?
- Reflect on the case study assessment: where did the zone recommendation feel uncertain, and which additional organizational information would have resolved that uncertainty?

Module 4 builds on Module 3 by translating the target zone declaration and investment commitments into a concrete, time-bound progression roadmap with leading indicators and decision points.
