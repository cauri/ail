## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, trainees will be able to:

1. **Prepare for a diagnostic workshop using the pre-workshop checklist and context analysis.** Complete all preparation steps -- logistics, discovery review, hypothesis formation -- so that the workshop itself can focus entirely on facilitation rather than setup.

2. **Open a workshop in a way that establishes psychological safety and self-assessment framing.** Communicate the purpose, ground rules, and dual reporting structure in a way that invites honest participation rather than defensive self-presentation.

3. **Facilitate zone-level assessment discussions by guiding without leading and probing without interrogating.** Ask questions that surface real behavioral evidence while keeping the team in control of their own assessment.

4. **Handle common facilitation challenges including defensive teams, overconfident teams, dominant voices, and the absent PM.** Recognize these patterns when they appear and respond with specific techniques that keep the assessment honest without creating conflict.

5. **Score workshop data accurately using zone questions and competency thresholds.** Apply the three-criterion competency threshold to workshop observations and team responses to produce a defensible, evidence-grounded zone and stage assessment.

## Content Outline

### Session 1: The Purpose and Design of the Diagnostic

**Why facilitated self-assessment, not audit.** The ACE diagnostic is not an external evaluation. No one is grading the team. The team is assessing themselves with a facilitator's help. This design choice is not a compromise -- it is the core of what makes the diagnostic work. Teams that feel evaluated perform; teams that feel supported reflect. Honest reflection produces better data than performed competence.

**The logic behind facilitated self-assessment.** External auditors see a team for a few hours. They observe what the team presents. Facilitated self-assessment surfaces what the team actually does, including under conditions the auditor never sees. The facilitator's job is to create conditions for that honesty, not to be the expert who tells the team where they stand.

**The dual reporting structure and why confidentiality matters.** Every ACE diagnostic produces two reports: a team report and a management report. The team report goes to the team. It contains specific scores, discussion themes, and improvement opportunities. The management report goes to organizational leadership. It contains systemic patterns and investment recommendations -- and never contains individual team attribution or specific team scores.

This separation is not a formality. It is what makes honest participation possible. If team members believe their individual scores will reach their manager, they will score aspirationally. If they believe the team's collective scores will be reported to leadership, they will score strategically. The dual reporting structure, explained clearly at the start of every workshop, is what breaks those incentives.

**How workshop design creates conditions for honesty.** Independent scoring before discussion prevents anchoring. Starting with low scorers during discussion prevents social pressure from silencing honest dissent. Framing every question around observed behavior rather than beliefs or intentions keeps discussion grounded. The facilitator does not tell the team what good looks like -- the team tells the facilitator what they actually do.

### Session 2: Pre-Workshop Preparation

**What the pre-workshop checklist covers.** The checklist is not optional infrastructure -- it is the difference between a productive workshop and a confused one. It covers three domains: logistics (room, timing, participant roster, materials), context analysis (discovery findings, organizational sensitivities, initial hypotheses), and participant preparation (pre-read sent, scoring method confirmed, sponsor briefed on dual reporting).

**Using discovery findings to calibrate expectations.** Before the workshop, the facilitator should have formed a working hypothesis about where the team is. This hypothesis comes from discovery interviews with the team lead and sponsor: What tools are in use? How widely? What does the team say about their AI practices? Are there known tensions or gaps?

The hypothesis is not a verdict. It is a starting point that focuses facilitation attention. If the discovery suggests the team has strong Zone 1 but inconsistent Zone 2, the facilitator knows to probe Zone 2 questions for evidence of "shared vs. individual" and "mandatory vs. aspirational." A facilitator without a hypothesis asks generic questions. A facilitator with a hypothesis asks specific ones.

**What can go wrong in preparation and how to prevent it.** The most common preparation failures: not confirming the dual reporting model with the sponsor before the workshop (this must be settled beforehand, not during); not identifying whether the manager will attend (their presence changes the dynamic -- see the pre-workshop checklist for the decision framework); scheduling during a crunch period or immediately after a production incident (stressed teams score their emotional state, not their practices); and skipping the pre-read (participants who arrive without context spend the first 20 minutes in orientation rather than assessment).

### Session 3: Opening the Workshop

**The first 15 minutes: what must be established.** The opening is the highest-leverage investment in the entire workshop. What gets established in the first 15 minutes determines whether the team engages honestly or performs for the facilitator. The opening must accomplish four things: explain what the diagnostic is (and what it is not), establish the ground rules, communicate the dual reporting structure, and give every participant a chance to ask questions before scoring begins.

**The self-assessment framing.** The single most important message to communicate: "This diagnostic exists to help the organization identify investments, not to judge your team's performance." This is not marketing language -- it is a behavioral instruction. It tells participants how to engage with the questions: report what you actually do, not what you aspire to do or what you know you should do.

Reinforce this framing with the competency definition: "Competency means what you do on a typical Tuesday, under normal pressure. Not your best day. Not when you are at a hackathon or on a Friday with nothing urgent. If a practice disappears when deadlines tighten, that is important information for the diagnostic, not a failure."

**Managing the sponsoring manager who wants to observe.** This situation is common and must be addressed directly. The manager's attendance -- even as a silent observer -- changes what team members are willing to say. The facilitator's job is to have this conversation with the sponsor before the workshop, not during it. The message: "I understand you want visibility into the team's assessment. The way to get the most honest assessment -- and therefore the most useful results -- is for the team to complete the diagnostic without you in the room. You will receive the management report, which contains everything you need for investment decisions."

If the manager attends despite this conversation, the facilitator acknowledges the dynamic openly with the full group: "I know having [manager name] here feels different. I want to name that directly. The goal of this session is your team's honest assessment, not a performance for leadership. [Manager name] is here to understand the process, not to evaluate individuals. Let me explain the dual reporting structure again so everyone is clear about what gets shared."

**Starting with psychological safety, not with the framework.** Facilitators trained on the ACE model sometimes open by explaining the framework in detail. This is a mistake. The team does not need to understand the full zone model to participate in the diagnostic. They need to feel safe being honest. Open with the purpose, the ground rules, and the reporting structure. Save framework explanation for the one paragraph of context that each zone's questions require.

**Distinguishing performed safety from genuine safety.** A well-run opening creates conditions for psychological safety. But there is a critical difference between a team that appears comfortable and a team that is genuinely willing to be honest. Performed safety looks like head-nodding, polite agreement, and quick 4-5 scores with no disagreement. Genuine safety looks like a team that is willing to disagree openly, name uncomfortable truths, and produce scores that span a range. Facilitators should be alert to the following signals that safety may be performed rather than genuine: (1) uniformly high scores with no variance and no hesitation; (2) discussion that stays abstract ("we generally do this") rather than specific ("last Tuesday when the deploy broke, I dropped AI tools and just fixed it manually"); (3) team members who look at a senior person before answering; (4) an absence of follow-up questions from participants about the confidentiality protections. If the facilitator suspects performed safety, the diagnosis-intervention cycle applies: diagnose the pattern, choose an intervention (e.g., pair discussion to reduce public exposure, written responses before verbal sharing, or directly naming the pattern: "I want to check in -- the scores are very uniform and the discussion is quite general. I want to make sure the conditions feel genuinely safe for candor, not just comfortable for consensus."), and assess whether the intervention shifts the dynamic.

### Session 4: Facilitation Techniques

**The Diagnosis-Intervention Cycle.** Every facilitation move in the ACE diagnostic follows a four-step cycle: observe, diagnose, intervene, reflect. The facilitator observes a behavioral pattern in the room (e.g., uniformly high scores with sparse evidence, one person answering for the team, a team member looking at the manager before responding). The facilitator forms a diagnosis -- a working hypothesis about what is happening and why (e.g., "social desirability bias is inflating scores," "the dominant voice is suppressing honest dissent," "the manager's presence is distorting self-report"). The facilitator then chooses an intervention -- a specific facilitation move designed to test the hypothesis and improve data quality (e.g., "probe for behavioral evidence," "redirect to quieter voices," "name the dynamic transparently"). Finally, the facilitator reflects on whether the intervention produced the expected effect and updates the hypothesis accordingly. This cycle is the core facilitation methodology. The specific techniques described below are all interventions within this cycle. Trainees who learn techniques without the diagnosis-intervention cycle will apply them mechanically -- using the same response to every challenge. Trainees who internalize the cycle can adapt in real time because they understand why each technique works, not just how to execute it.

**The difference between facilitating and leading.** A facilitator asks questions that help the team see their own practices clearly. A leader tells the team what good practice looks like and asks them to rate themselves against it. These produce very different data. "How often do all of you use AI tools when you're under deadline pressure?" is a facilitation question. "Best practice is to use AI tools even under pressure -- how often does your team do that?" is a leading question disguised as assessment.

Every question the facilitator asks during discussion should follow the same pattern: anchor to observable behavior, anchor to recent time periods, and leave the evaluation to the team. "Think about the last sprint. Can someone describe a specific moment when the team's AGENTS.md file was actually the thing that guided the AI?" is a facilitation question. "Would you say your AGENTS.md is working well?" is a leading question.

**Probing for evidence: the standard follow-up.** When a team member gives a score, the facilitator's default follow-up is: "What does that look like on a typical Tuesday?" This question does three things simultaneously: it anchors the response to observable behavior, it invokes the competency framing (typical Tuesday, not best day), and it invites specificity without demanding it. A team member who cannot produce a typical-Tuesday example for a score of 4 or 5 is revealing something important. The facilitator does not press aggressively -- they note the absence of examples and return to it when appropriate.

Other evidence-probing techniques: "Can you walk me through the last time your team was up against a hard deadline? Did the practice hold?" // "You scored this a 3. What would have to change for this to be a 4 -- what does the gap look like?" // "I'm hearing two different descriptions of the same practice. Can you help me understand when you each see what you're describing?"

**Surfacing disagreement constructively.** High variance in scores is a diagnostic signal. When the distribution shows a 2 and a 5 on the same question, the facilitator's job is not to resolve the disagreement -- it is to understand it. Start by asking the low scorer to explain: "I see a range of scores here. I want to start with the lower end. What are you seeing that led you there?" This is counterintuitive -- most facilitation instincts reach for the confident high scorer first. Starting with the low scorer prevents social pressure from suppressing honest dissent.

**Reading the room.** Body language and deflection patterns carry information. A team where most people score quickly and confidently but one person pauses and erases suggests internal disagreement. A manager-adjacent participant who looks at the manager before answering suggests the manager's attendance is distorting the assessment. A team that gives identical scores across all questions is likely anchoring. A participant who consistently talks about "we" but uses the same examples every time may be describing one other person's behavior, not the team's. None of these is definitive -- all are cues to probe.

**The five whys adapted for competency probing.** When a team claims a behavior is consistent, the facilitator can use a gentle version of five whys to test whether the claim is knowledge-based or behavior-based. "You said the team always runs the linter before committing. What happens when someone is in a hurry and the linter catches something annoying?" // "And when that happens, does the team member push through the lint fix or skip it?" // "And what happens to the PR if they skipped it?" The goal is not to catch the team in an inconsistency -- it is to find the boundary of where the behavior holds.

### Session 5: Zone-by-Zone Facilitation

**Zone 1 facilitation: what to watch for.** Zone 1 is about individual behavior. The key question is not "does your team use AI tools" -- most teams will say yes -- but "does every member of this team use AI tools habitually, even the ones who are skeptical, even under pressure?" Watch for the hero developer pattern: one or two enthusiastic AI users who answer for the team, while quieter members have much lower usage. Ask individuals directly. "Let me check in with everyone separately. [Name], what does your AI tool usage look like on a day when you're in the middle of a complex debugging session?"

**Zone 2 facilitation: shared vs. individual, mandatory vs. aspirational.** Zone 2 is about team-level behavior, and the two critical distinctions are: shared vs. individual (does the whole team use the same setup, or does each person have their own), and mandatory vs. aspirational (are feedback loops enforced by the CI/CD pipeline, or are they polite recommendations?). Watch for the AGENTS.md-as-one-time-setup pattern: a team that has AGENTS.md committed but has not touched it in months. Probe: "When did you last update your AGENTS.md? What prompted it? What was in the retrospective?"

**Probing role-based score variance.** When Zone 2 scores show high variance, check whether the variance breaks along role lines. If engineering scores high but PM, design, and QA score low, this is a significant diagnostic signal: the "shared workflow" may be shared only among engineers while other roles remain outside it. Name this pattern explicitly: "I'm noticing that the engineering scores are clustered around 4-5 but the PM and QA scores are around 2-3. That gap suggests the shared workflow may not be truly shared across the full team yet. Let's explore what that looks like."

**Facilitator notes from the questionnaires.** The zone questionnaires include facilitator notes on each question that flag common patterns. The most important patterns:
- Zone 1, core metric: teams often score this high because they conflate "has used AI" with "uses AI habitually." Probe for under-pressure behavior.
- Zone 2, Q1 (core metric): teams often score high because they have the workflow documented but not habitual. Ask: "During the last production incident, did the team use Plan/Code/Verify, or did they go straight to 'fix this now'?"
- Zone 2, Q3 (mandatory feedback loops): teams with a linter in CI/CD score high. Teams where the linter is optional or bypassed should score lower. Ask: "What happens if someone commits code that fails the linter?"

**Zone 3/4 facilitation: include all role transformations.** When introducing Zone 3, do not describe it solely as an engineering identity shift. The spoken introduction must include the parallel role transformations: PMs as behavioral specifiers, designers as specification authors, QA as evaluation pipeline specialists. These are not optional additions -- they are essential to the production pipeline that Zone 3 describes. Similarly, when introducing Zone 4, name the multi-role factory team: engineers as factory designers, PMs as portfolio-level specifiers, designers as specification layer owners, and QA as evaluation infrastructure operators. A facilitator who describes Zone 3 or Zone 4 in exclusively engineering terms will produce assessments that miss critical gaps in non-engineering role transformation.

**When to stop.** Most teams will not reach Zone 3. The facilitator should assess Zone 3 only if Zone 2 produces scores consistent with Established or Exemplary. If Zone 2 scores are in the Emerging or Developing range, stop at Zone 2 and note in the report that Zone 3 assessment was not conducted because Zone 2 foundations are not yet stable. This is not a failure -- it is accurate information.

### Session 6: Scoring and Interpretation

**How to score from workshop observations and team responses.** Scoring in ACE uses the Composite + Consistency competency threshold. The three criteria are: (1) the zone composite average (mean of all question composites) is 4.7 or higher; (2) the standard deviation across all individual responses is 0.5 or lower; (3) no single question composite falls below 4.0.

A team meets all three criteria only when competency is genuinely universal and consistent. Missing any criterion determines the stage: scores in the 2.0-2.9 range is Emerging; scores in the 3.0-3.9 range is Developing; scores in the 4.0-4.9 range without meeting all three threshold criteria is Established; all three criteria met is Exemplary.

**The difference between self-report scores and facilitated scores.** Self-report scores are what team members put on the form before discussion. Facilitated scores are the scores that emerge after evidence-probing discussion, when the team has had the opportunity to reconsider their initial responses in light of behavioral evidence. These are often different, and the difference should be made transparent rather than resolved privately by the facilitator.

When the facilitator observes a pattern where scores are high but behavioral evidence is sparse, the facilitator names this pattern to the group -- directed at the pattern, not at individuals: 'I notice several people scored 4 or 5 on this question, but when I asked for specific recent examples, it was hard for anyone to produce one. That gap between the score and the evidence is worth exploring together. What do you think is happening here?' This gives the team the opportunity to recalibrate their own scores based on reflection, rather than the facilitator substituting private judgment for the team's self-assessment.

If, after this transparent exploration, the team maintains their original scores, the facilitator has two options: (1) accept the team's scores and note in the report that the facilitation discussion produced limited behavioral evidence to support the scores, letting the reader draw their own conclusion; or (2) report both the team's self-report scores and the facilitator's assessment based on discussion evidence, with transparent reasoning for the discrepancy. Option 2 is preferred because it preserves the integrity of the self-assessment while giving the report reader the information they need to form their own judgment.

What the facilitator should not do is privately replace the team's scores with their own assessment without sharing the reasoning. This violates the self-assessment principle the framework is built on.

**When to challenge a score vs. accept it.** Challenge a score when: the discussion reveals no concrete behavioral evidence for a high score; the team's description of their practice matches a lower-frequency behavior than the score suggests; or there is a clear discrepancy between what one or two vocal members claim and what quieter members acknowledge. Accept a score when: the team produces specific, recent, concrete behavioral examples; multiple team members independently describe consistent behavior; and the facilitator's own observations during the session are consistent with the score.

**Applying competency thresholds.** The thresholds are criteria, not a formula. A team with a composite average of 4.8 but a standard deviation of 0.7 is Established, not Exemplary. The facilitator must check all three criteria independently — high composite average, low variance, and no weak-link questions.

### Session 7: Common Facilitation Challenges

**Defensive team: "We use AI all the time, this is all 5s."** This is a self-protection pattern, not a factual report. The facilitation response is not to argue -- it is to probe for specificity. "Let me try something. Instead of scores, let's start with a story. Can someone walk me through yesterday? From when you sat down to work until end of day -- when did you use AI tools, and what for?" Behavioral stories are much harder to inflate than numerical self-reports. One specific story from one team member will often reveal a more accurate picture than five minutes of defensive claims.

**Overconfident team: scores higher than behaviors warrant.** This is common in teams with one or two high-enthusiasm AI users who speak for the team. The response is to individualize the assessment. "I want to make sure I'm capturing the full team's experience. Let's go around and each person give me one specific example from the past week." When the enthusiast speaks first and others cannot produce comparable examples, the picture becomes clearer.

**The absent PM problem.** Zone 2 requires PM participation in AI-augmented workflows. A diagnostic where the PM is absent produces an incomplete Zone 2 picture. If the PM is unavailable, note this explicitly in the report. Do not rate Zone 2 PM-specific questions based on engineer reports of what the PM does -- those secondhand reports are unreliable. Schedule a follow-up session with the PM or note the gap and flag it as a risk in the report.

**The dominant voice.** One person answers for the team on every question. The facilitation response: use independent scoring (scores submitted before discussion begins) and actively solicit specific other voices. "Before we discuss that, I want to hear from [quieter team member]. What score did you give, and what were you thinking?" If the dominant voice interrupts: "[Name], I want to make sure we hear from everyone. Let's get [other name]'s perspective first."

**The honest outlier.** One team member scores significantly lower than everyone else. Other team members push back. This is one of the highest-value facilitation moments in the entire diagnostic. The honest outlier is probably seeing something real. The facilitation response: protect the outlier, explore their perspective, and do not let the group pressure them into recanting. "I want to stay with [name]'s perspective for a minute, because it's different from the rest of the group and that difference is worth understanding. [Name], can you tell us more about what you're seeing?"

**Technical interruptions in virtual settings.** Have a backup plan. If the scoring tool fails, switch to a Google Form or paper tally. If audio quality degrades, switch to chat-based discussion. Brief the group at the start of remote sessions: "If we have technical issues, here is what we will do." Do not let a 5-minute tech problem derail a 90-minute session.

## Learning Activities

### Activity 1: Pre-Workshop Preparation Walkthrough

**Format:** Individual exercise with group debrief, 30 minutes

Each trainee receives a scenario packet: a one-page discovery summary for a mid-size product team (team size, tech stack, sponsor relationship, known sensitivities), a logistics briefing (room, timing, remote participants), and a blank pre-workshop checklist. Working individually for 15 minutes, trainees must:

1. Complete every item on the checklist using the scenario information, noting where the scenario leaves gaps that would require follow-up before the real workshop.
2. Form a working hypothesis about the team's likely zone and stage, citing specific evidence from the discovery summary.
3. Identify two participant roster risks (e.g., the engineering manager plans to attend, the PM is unavailable) and write the specific message they would send to address each risk.
4. Draft the three-sentence email to the sponsor confirming the dual reporting structure.

After individual work, the group debriefs: What did people miss on the checklist? Where did working hypotheses diverge, and what discovery evidence would resolve the disagreement? What language did people use in the dual-reporting confirmation -- did any version inadvertently make it sound optional?

**Key learning point:** Trainees who skip the hypothesis step discover during debrief that they cannot explain what they plan to probe during the workshop. Preparation without a hypothesis is logistics without facilitation intent.

### Activity 2: Role-Play -- The Defensive Team

**Format:** Pairs exercise, 45 minutes

One trainee plays the facilitator. One trainee plays an engineering manager who deflects honest assessment: "We're pretty advanced with AI here -- the team uses it constantly. I think most of these would be 4s and 5s." The persona card includes hidden truths: two team members barely use AI tools, the AGENTS.md was set up once six months ago and never touched, and feedback loops are aspirational rather than mandatory.

The facilitating trainee must surface honest information despite the defensive framing without confronting the manager or alienating them. After 15 minutes, pairs switch roles with a new scenario. After both rounds, the group debriefs: what techniques worked? What felt confrontational? What was left on the table?

**Variant: Failed first intervention.** In at least one round, the persona card should include an instruction for the role-player to resist the facilitator's first intervention attempt -- the defensive posture does not shift, or it shifts in the wrong direction (e.g., the manager becomes more guarded). This forces the facilitating trainee to recognize that the initial diagnosis or intervention was insufficient, update their hypothesis about what is driving the defensiveness, and try a different approach. The debrief for this variant should specifically explore: How did the trainee recognize the first intervention had failed? What updated diagnosis did they form? How did the second approach differ from the first, and why?

### Activity 3: Evidence Probing Practice

**Format:** Small groups, 30 minutes

Each group receives a set of 5-6 vague workshop responses -- the kind that sound like they belong to a high score but contain no behavioral specificity. Examples: "We definitely use Plan/Code/Verify as our workflow." // "Our AGENTS.md has everything the AI needs to work in our codebase." // "We do code review on AI output, just like any other code." // "The team is really bought into AI tools."

Groups must generate 2-3 follow-up probing questions for each response. Questions must be non-leading, behavior-anchored, and specific to the claim. Groups share their best probing questions and discuss: Which questions would produce the most diagnostic value? Which risked leading the witness?

### Activity 4: Score Calibration Exercise

**Format:** Full group, 30 minutes

Each trainee independently reviews a set of workshop notes: the raw individual scores from six team members across Zone 1 and Zone 2 questions, plus the facilitator's observation notes from the discussion (two pages of qualitative notes including specific examples, deflections, and inter-team disagreements). Working individually, trainees apply the three-criterion competency threshold to determine:

1. The Zone 1 competency stage with supporting evidence.
2. The Zone 2 competency stage with supporting evidence.
3. Any items where the observation notes suggest the scores are misleading.

The group then compares their assessments. The facilitator leads discussion around disagreements, emphasizing that scoring requires judgment and that the richest interpretive questions arise when qualitative evidence and quantitative scores conflict.

### Activity 5: Mock Workshop Facilitation

**Format:** Full cohort, 90 minutes

One trainee facilitates a 30-minute mock Zone 1 assessment. Other trainees play team members using persona cards that include: role (engineer, PM, designer), AI usage level, one or two "hidden truths" that will only emerge with skillful probing, and a behavioral tendency (dominant voice, honest outlier, quiet deflector, over-enthusiastic self-reporter).

The mock workshop follows full structure: framing, independent scoring, facilitated discussion, brief retrospective. Trainees rotate the facilitator role so each person facilitates at least one 10-minute segment. After the mock workshop, the full group debriefs using specific examples from the session.

**Formal Scoring.** The mock workshop is a scored developmental assessment, not just a learning activity. The master facilitator scores each trainee's facilitation segment using a simplified version of the Facilitation Observation Rubric covering three dimensions: Evidence Probing, Variance Handling, and Energy/Voice Management. Scores are shared with each trainee individually after the activity, along with specific behavioral observations. Mock workshop scores are developmental -- they identify areas for focused improvement before the supervised facilitation rather than serving as a pass/fail gate. Trainees who receive 'Not Yet Competent' ratings on any dimension should work with their cohort peers on targeted practice in that area before the supervised facilitation.

At least one persona card should include a power dynamic element -- for example, a team member who is less experienced and visibly defers to a more senior member, or a team member whose role (PM, QA) gives them less organizational status than the engineers in the room. This gives trainees practice navigating the equity and inclusion dynamics they will encounter in real workshops.

## Assessment

### Facilitation Observation Rubric

The Facilitation Observation Rubric is used during the supervised facilitation component of the practicum. The rubric uses a Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scale (BARS) across six dimensions. For each dimension, five performance levels are defined with specific observable behaviors: Not Yet Competent, Developing, Competent, Advanced, and Exemplary. The minimum standard for certification is 'Competent' on all six dimensions. The five-level scale provides finer discrimination at the certification boundary, enabling master facilitators to distinguish between trainees who are approaching competence (Developing) and those who have achieved it -- a distinction the rubric must make reliably for certification decisions.

**Dimension 1: Opening Effectiveness** (framing, safety establishment, dual reporting explanation)
- *Not Yet Competent:* Reads the opening script without adapting to the room's energy. Does not check for understanding of the dual reporting structure. Fails to establish self-assessment framing before scoring begins. Team members begin scoring uncertain about what the process is or who will see results.
- *Developing:* Communicates the key elements (purpose, ground rules, dual reporting) but in a rote or rushed manner. Checks for understanding but does not pause long enough for genuine questions. Adapts tone somewhat but misses signals of discomfort or confusion in the room.
- *Competent:* Communicates the self-assessment framing clearly and checks for understanding. Explains the dual reporting structure and invites questions. Establishes ground rules and adapts tone to the room's energy. Team members begin scoring with a shared understanding of the purpose and confidentiality protections.
- *Advanced:* Tailors the opening based on discovery findings and the specific team context. Addresses likely concerns before they are raised. Reads the room's energy and adjusts pacing accordingly. Creates genuine space for questions and responds to them substantively.
- *Exemplary:* Adapts the opening to specific organizational context from discovery findings. Names potential concerns proactively ('I know this was initiated by leadership, and I want to address what that means for you directly'). Creates conditions where team members visibly relax and engage before scoring begins.

**Dimension 2: Evidence Probing** (question quality, non-leading approach, specificity of follow-up)
- *Not Yet Competent:* Accepts high self-report scores without follow-up. Asks leading questions that suggest the expected answer ('So your team does use Plan/Code/Verify consistently, right?'). Does not probe for specific behavioral examples. Treats scores as data rather than conversation starters.
- *Developing:* Attempts follow-up questions but they are sometimes leading or generic. Probes high scores but not low scores (or vice versa). Recognizes when evidence is thin but does not know how to dig deeper without confrontation.
- *Competent:* Follows up scores with behavior-anchored probes ('What does that look like on a typical Tuesday?'). Asks non-leading questions. Notes when a participant cannot produce a specific example. Probes symmetrically -- challenges both high scores lacking evidence and low scores that may reflect underestimation.
- *Advanced:* Uses multiple probing techniques (typical Tuesday, five-whys, pressure test) and selects among them based on the situation. Probes for the boundary of habitual behavior. Notices when answers shift from specific to abstract and redirects to specifics.
- *Exemplary:* Adapts probing strategy based on team dynamics and discovery findings. Uses the five-whys technique effectively to test the boundary of habitual behavior. Surfaces discrepancies between self-report and discussion evidence without confrontation. Follows threads that were not anticipated in the preparation.

**Dimension 3: Variance Handling** (surfacing, exploring, and using disagreement productively)
- *Not Yet Competent:* Ignores variance in scores or resolves it by averaging. Allows high scorers to dominate the explanation of variance. Does not invite low scorers to share their perspective first. Treats disagreement as a problem to resolve rather than diagnostic data to explore.
- *Developing:* Notices variance and comments on it but does not explore it systematically. May invite different perspectives but defaults to the first explanation offered. Uncomfortable with sustained disagreement.
- *Competent:* Identifies high-variance items and draws explicit attention to them. Invites low scorers to speak first. Protects outlier perspectives from group pressure. Uses variance to generate insight about different experiences within the team. Does not prematurely resolve disagreements.
- *Advanced:* Identifies patterns in variance (role-based, tenure-based) and names them. Holds space for sustained disagreement without rushing to resolution. Uses variance data across questions to build a diagnostic picture of the team.
- *Exemplary:* Identifies role-based, tenure-based, and context-based patterns in variance. Names the pattern explicitly ('I notice the engineering scores cluster around 4-5 but PM and QA cluster around 2-3'). Uses variance to surface structural issues that individual scores would not reveal.

**Dimension 4: Energy and Voice Management** (dominant voice, honest outlier, quiet contributors)
- *Not Yet Competent:* Allows one or two voices to dominate the discussion without redirecting. Does not actively solicit quieter team members. Fails to protect the honest outlier when the group pushes back. Loses control of the conversational flow during heated moments.
- *Developing:* Notices when one voice dominates but redirects hesitantly or too late. Solicits quieter voices but with generic invitations ('Anyone else?') rather than specific ones. Recognizes the honest outlier but does not actively protect their perspective.
- *Competent:* Manages dominant voices through direct but respectful redirection. Actively solicits input from quiet team members by name. Protects outlier perspectives from group pressure ('I want to stay with this perspective for a moment'). Maintains conversational flow while allowing productive tension.
- *Advanced:* Proactively manages energy flow before imbalances become entrenched. Uses multiple participation formats (written, pair, round-robin) to create equity. Reads body language and adjusts in real time.
- *Exemplary:* Reads non-verbal cues (body language, hesitation, eye contact toward manager) and responds appropriately. Creates multiple participation channels (verbal, written, pair discussion) to accommodate different communication preferences. Recognizes when silence is productive (thinking) versus suppressive (withholding).

**Dimension 5: Role Inclusion and Equity Awareness** (active solicitation of cross-role perspectives; awareness of power dynamics)
- *Not Yet Competent:* Facilitates the diagnostic as an engineering exercise. Does not actively solicit PM, design, or QA perspectives. Does not notice or address power dynamics that may suppress honest participation (e.g., junior members deferring to seniors, members looking to manager before answering).
- *Developing:* Aware that non-engineering perspectives matter and asks PMs and designers to contribute, but does so late in the discussion or as an afterthought. Notices the most obvious power dynamics but does not intervene effectively.
- *Competent:* Actively solicits perspectives from all roles represented in the room. Names role-based score variance when it appears. Notices observable power dynamics (deference patterns, eye contact toward authority figures) and addresses them directly or through facilitation technique (e.g., individual written responses before group discussion).
- *Advanced:* Integrates cross-role perspectives throughout the discussion rather than as a separate check. Uses discovery findings to anticipate likely role-based dynamics. Adjusts facilitation format proactively when power dynamics appear (e.g., switches to written responses without being prompted).
- *Exemplary:* Adapts facilitation approach based on the specific power dynamics of this team, informed by discovery findings. Creates conditions where team members with less organizational power contribute as fully as those with more. Surfaces the equity implications of AI adoption when relevant to the assessment (e.g., 'Who on this team has had the most access to AI tools and training time? Who has had the least?').

**Dimension 6: Diagnostic Reasoning** (hypothesis formation, inference testing, adaptive intervention)
- *Not Yet Competent:* Follows the workshop script as a sequence without adapting to what is happening in the room. Cannot articulate why they chose a particular probe or intervention when asked during debrief. Treats all facilitation challenges with the same generic response.
- *Developing:* Shows awareness that adaptation is needed and attempts to form hypotheses, but hypotheses are vague or untested. Can articulate reasoning in debrief with prompting but does not do so spontaneously. Applies the diagnosis-intervention cycle inconsistently.
- *Competent:* Forms hypotheses about group dynamics during the workshop (e.g., 'I think this team is anchoring to the dominant voice'). Tests hypotheses through specific interventions. Can articulate their diagnostic reasoning during the debrief: 'At this moment, I noticed X, I hypothesized Y, and I chose to do Z because...' Differentiates between at least two possible diagnoses for a given behavioral pattern.
- *Advanced:* Applies the diagnosis-intervention cycle fluidly across multiple simultaneous dynamics. Updates hypotheses in real time as new evidence emerges. Can articulate reasoning without prompting during debrief. Recognizes common organizational defensive routines (e.g., abstract language to avoid specifics, unanimity as social pressure).
- *Exemplary:* Demonstrates fluid diagnostic reasoning in real time -- adapts approach mid-discussion based on emerging evidence. Recognizes organizational defensive routines (Argyris) and responds appropriately. Shares diagnostic reasoning transparently with the group when appropriate ('I notice that when I ask about pressure resilience, the energy in the room shifts. I want to name that and check: is this question touching something that feels risky to discuss?').

**Structured Debrief Protocol for Supervised Facilitations**

After each supervised facilitation, the master facilitator conducts a structured debrief following this sequence:

1. **Trainee self-assessment (5 minutes):** 'Before I share my observations, tell me how you think it went. What worked? What would you do differently? Walk me through your reasoning at one or two key moments.'
2. **Diagnostic reasoning review (10 minutes):** The master facilitator asks the trainee to articulate their diagnostic reasoning at 2-3 specific moments identified during observation: 'At the point when [specific moment], what were you thinking? What did you notice? What hypotheses did you form? Why did you choose to do what you did?'
3. **Rubric-anchored feedback (15-20 minutes):** The master facilitator shares specific observations organized by the six rubric dimensions, using the behavioral anchors to locate the trainee's performance. Feedback uses the format: 'I observed [specific behavior]. On the rubric, that maps to [level] because [explanation]. Here is what [Competent/Exemplary] would look like in that moment: [specific alternative].'
4. **Improvement targets (5 minutes):** The trainee and master facilitator collaboratively identify 2-3 specific improvement targets for the second facilitation.
5. **Written summary:** The master facilitator provides a written summary of the debrief within 48 hours, organized by rubric dimension with specific behavioral observations and improvement targets.

This protocol ensures consistent debrief quality across master facilitators and creates a documented learning trajectory for each trainee.

**Inter-rater reliability for BARS scoring.** Because the Facilitation Observation Rubric is used for certification decisions, facilitator scoring consistency should be monitored through periodic calibration exercises. At least once per cohort cycle, all active master facilitators should independently score the same recorded facilitation segment using the rubric, then compare and discuss their ratings. Discrepancies of two or more levels on any dimension should be discussed until the group reaches a shared interpretation of the behavioral anchors. These calibration exercises serve two purposes: they maintain scoring consistency across master facilitators, and they surface places where the rubric's behavioral anchors may need refinement because they are being interpreted differently.

### Scoring Accuracy Assessment

**Format:** In-session or take-home, 30 minutes

Trainees receive a complete set of workshop data from a fictional diagnostic: individual scores from seven team members across all Zone 1 and Zone 2 questions, plus two pages of facilitator observation notes that include specific behavioral examples, one clear instance where a team member's discussion evidence contradicts their self-reported score, and a core metric response pattern where six of seven members scored 5 but one scored 3.

Trainees must:

1. Determine the Zone 1 competency stage by applying all three criteria of the competency threshold. Show the work: state whether each criterion is met or not met, with the specific numbers.
2. Determine the Zone 2 competency stage using the same method.
3. Identify the item where the observation notes suggest the self-report score is misleading, explain what the facilitated score should be, and justify the adjustment.
4. Write a two-sentence summary of the zone assessment as it would appear in the opening paragraph of the team report.

Scoring gives equal weight to correct threshold application (did the trainee check all three criteria independently, not just compute an average?), evidence-based judgment (did the trainee use the observation notes to adjust the misleading score?), and report-quality writing (is the summary accurate, concise, and non-judgmental?).

**Acceptable performance standard.** The trainee must demonstrate all of the following to pass this assessment: (1) correctly identify whether each of the three competency threshold criteria is met or not met for both zones, with the specific numbers shown (no more than one computational error across both zones); (2) identify the correct competency stage for at least one of the two zones, with reasoning that references the stage definitions; (3) correctly identify the misleading self-report item and provide a facilitated score adjustment that is directionally correct and grounded in specific observation note evidence; and (4) produce a team report summary that accurately reflects the zone assessment and avoids judgmental or maturity-model language. Trainees should show their work and verify their own calculations before submitting. If a trainee makes a computational error but catches it through self-checking (crossed out and corrected), this demonstrates the self-verification behavior required in real-world scoring where no answer key exists. Uncaught computational errors should receive feedback that specifically addresses the self-checking gap, not just the arithmetic. Trainees who miss the threshold application or the misleading item identification should receive targeted practice with additional scoring exercises before the supervised facilitation.

### Written Reflection

**Format:** Take-home, 1-2 pages

Describe one facilitation challenge from the mock workshop (or from a prior facilitation experience) and explain how you would handle it differently now. The reflection must include: a specific description of what happened, the facilitation technique you would apply, why that technique is appropriate to this specific challenge, and what outcome you would expect.
