This guide walks through the process of selecting the right ACE target zone for your organization. The goal is not to reach the highest zone possible but to choose the zone that best serves your strategic position, risk tolerance, and investment capacity.

## Prerequisites

Before starting this process, you need:

- Completed ACE diagnostic results establishing your current zone and competency stage
- Access to organizational leadership who can discuss strategic priorities and investment capacity
- Understanding of the [progressive competency model](/toolkit/progressive-competency-model) -- how zone progression works and why organizations choose their stopping point

## Step 1: Establish Your Current Zone

Run the ACE diagnostic to determine where your organization is today. The diagnostic produces two key outputs:

- **Current zone:** Which zone's proficiencies does the organization practice habitually?
- **Competency stage within that zone:** Emerging, Developing, Established, or Exemplary?

If your current zone is 0 or 1, your typical near-term target is Zone 2 -- the competitive baseline for modern software teams. The question of whether to continue to Zone 3 or Zone 4 becomes relevant after you have achieved at least Zone 2 proficiency. Each transition along the progression deserves its own strategic analysis.

If you are already at Zone 2, proceed to Step 2 to determine whether Zones 3 or 4 are appropriate targets.

## Step 2: Assess Organizational Context

Every zone transition deserves strategic analysis. For organizations at Zone 2 considering further progression, evaluate across three dimensions:

### Risk Appetite

Zone 3 and 4 transitions require structural changes that are difficult and expensive to reverse. Ask:

- Has our organization successfully managed large-scale structural changes in the past (role restructuring, team reorganization, new career ladders)?
- Can we tolerate a 6-12 month period of reduced productivity as teams adapt to fundamentally different working patterns?
- Do we have the organizational resilience to absorb the disruption of changing engineering roles, compensation structures, and hiring profiles?
- What is the cost of failure? If the transition does not produce expected results, can we course-correct without significant organizational damage?

**Low risk appetite** suggests targeting deep Zone 2 competency rather than Zone 3. There is substantial value in becoming deeply competent within Zone 2, and the risk profile is significantly lower.

### Investment Capacity

Each zone transition requires progressively larger organizational investments. Ask:

- Do we have budget for the structural changes required (not just tool licenses, but role restructuring, compensation adjustments, specialized hiring)?
- Can leadership dedicate sustained attention to this initiative over 12-24 months, or will it compete with other priorities?
- Do we have internal change management capability, or do we need external consulting support?
- Can we sustain the investment through inevitable setbacks and slow periods without pulling funding?

**Limited investment capacity** suggests prioritizing depth within your current zone over advancing to the next. A deeply competent Zone 2 organization outperforms a struggling Zone 3 organization.

### Strategic Need

Not every organization benefits from AI-native development capabilities. Ask:

- Is AI-augmented development a source of competitive advantage in our industry, or is software a supporting function?
- Are our competitors investing in Zone 3+ capabilities, creating competitive pressure to match?
- Does our product strategy require the engineering leverage that Zone 3 or 4 provides, or can we achieve our product goals with Zone 2 capabilities?
- Would the engineering talent we need to attract and retain expect Zone 3+ practices, or is Zone 2 sufficient for our talent market?

**Low strategic need** suggests that investment in Zone 3 or 4 carries opportunity cost -- those resources might deliver more value applied elsewhere.

## Step 3: Apply the Decision Framework

Use the following framework to guide your zone selection:

### Target Zone 2 (from Zone 0 or 1)

Zone 2 is the typical near-term target for most organizations -- the competitive baseline. While the investment is modest relative to later transitions, organizations should assess competency and plan specific investments. If you are not yet at Zone 2, this is the natural next step.

### Target deep Zone 2 competency (already at Zone 2)

Choose this when:
- Your risk appetite for structural change is low
- Investment capacity is constrained
- Software development is important but not your primary competitive differentiator
- Your competitive position does not require AI-native development capabilities

This is a strong, defensible position. Focus on moving from Developing to Established or from Established to Exemplary within Zone 2.

### Target Zone 3

Choose this when:
- You have successfully managed structural organizational changes before
- You can sustain 12-18 months of investment in role restructuring and team reorganization
- Your product strategy benefits from significantly higher engineering leverage
- You need to attract and retain talent that expects AI-native development practices
- Competitors are moving toward Zone 3 capabilities

### Target Zone 4

Choose this when:
- AI-augmented development is a core strategic differentiator for your organization
- You have executive-level sponsorship and dedicated capital allocation
- You can acquire and retain specialized AI/ML talent
- Your market position rewards proprietary AI capabilities
- You are willing to make a multi-year commitment with uncertain but potentially transformative returns

## Step 4: Build the Investment Case

Once you have identified a target zone, build the investment case for leadership. Frame the transition as a business investment, not an AI training initiative. See [How to Present to Leadership](/toolkit/present-to-leadership) for detailed guidance.

The investment case should include:

- Current zone and competency stage (from diagnostic)
- Target zone and rationale (from context assessment)
- Specific organizational investments required (from zone reference documentation)
- Expected benefits and timeline
- Leading indicators that will signal progress
- Reassessment cadence

## Warning Signs

### Targeting Too High

- Leadership is excited about Zone 4 but has not invested in reaching Zone 2
- The target zone was chosen based on industry hype rather than strategic analysis
- The organization has a history of launching ambitious initiatives that lose support after 6 months
- No one has articulated what specific business outcomes the higher zone will produce

### Targeting Too Low

- Competitors are visibly outpacing you in AI-augmented development and it is affecting your market position
- Your engineering talent is leaving for organizations with more advanced AI practices
- Your product roadmap requires capabilities that your current zone cannot deliver
- Leadership's risk aversion is based on unfamiliarity with AI rather than genuine strategic analysis

## Next Steps

After choosing your target zone:

1. Create a progression roadmap. See [How to Create a Progression Roadmap](/toolkit/create-progression-roadmap).
2. Present the plan to leadership. See [How to Present to Leadership](/toolkit/present-to-leadership).
3. Schedule the first reassessment (typically 3-6 months after beginning implementation).
