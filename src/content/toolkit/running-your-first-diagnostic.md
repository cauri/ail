This tutorial walks you through the full arc of an ACE diagnostic workshop — from preparation to follow-up. It is written as a learning journey, not a checklist. You will understand not just what to do at each step, but why it matters and what to watch for.

This tutorial covers ground taught in depth in [Module 2: Diagnostic Facilitation Skills](/training/module-2-diagnostic-facilitation) of the training program. If you have not completed Module 2, this guide provides orientation but is not a substitute for the full module, which includes learning activities, role-play practice, and supervised assessment. Use this as a preview before training or as a refresher before engagements.

If you have not yet read the [Facilitator Guide](/toolkit/facilitator-guide), start there. It covers the broader context of your role and the engagement lifecycle. This tutorial focuses specifically on the diagnostic workshop itself.

## Before the Workshop

### Prepare Yourself

Your most important preparation is not logistical -- it is mental. You need to walk into the room (or video call) as someone who deeply understands the ACE model and can explain it conversationally.

Review the zone reference documents ([Zone 1](/toolkit/zone-1-augmenting), [Zone 2](/toolkit/zone-2-integrating), [Zone 3](/toolkit/zone-3-accelerating), [Zone 4](/toolkit/zone-4-industrializing)) for whichever zones you will be assessing. Most workshops assess Zone 1 and Zone 2. For each zone, know the core metric, the key proficiencies, and the organizational investments. You do not need to memorize every detail, but you should be able to explain what each zone represents in plain language, without jargon.

Review the context you gathered during the Discovery phase. What did you learn about this team in stakeholder interviews? What AI tools are they using? What is their delivery culture like? What sensitivities did you pick up? This context lets you ask informed follow-up questions rather than generic ones. A facilitator who says "tell me more about that" is adequate. A facilitator who says "you mentioned your team switched to Cursor last quarter -- has that changed how you approach code review?" is excellent.

Review the [scoring thresholds](/toolkit/scoring-thresholds) so you can quickly interpret score distributions during the workshop. You will need to read a score distribution and identify patterns -- high variance, bimodal splits, uniform scores -- in real time.

### Set Up the Room

**In-person:** Arrange seating in a circle or U-shape. Avoid classroom-style rows -- the team needs to see each other during discussion. You need to be able to move around the room, not be stuck behind a podium. Post zone labels and question numbers on the wall as visual anchors. Place scoring forms, pens, and a scale reference card (1 = Never through 5 = Always) at each seat.

**Remote:** Open the video call 10 minutes early. Test screen sharing and the scoring tool (anonymous poll, shared form, or private message submission). Have a backup plan if the polling tool fails -- participants can DM you their scores. Prepare a shared document for live score display.

### Prepare Materials

You need: scoring forms (one per participant per zone), a score tally sheet, a timer, your facilitation notes, and the [discussion prompts](/toolkit/discussion-prompts). The [pre-workshop checklist](/toolkit/pre-workshop-checklist) covers the full list.

### Common First-Time Mistakes to Avoid

- **Under-preparing discussion prompts.** The scores take 15 minutes. The discussion takes 30. If you have not thought about how to open discussion on each zone, you will flounder. Prepare 2-3 probing questions per zone in advance.
- **Forgetting to confirm anonymity with the sponsor.** If you have not explicitly confirmed the dual reporting model with the organizational sponsor, do so now. Do not wait until a team member asks "who sees these scores?" and you are unsure.
- **Scheduling too tight.** Leave 15-20 minutes of buffer. Discussions run long. If you are back-to-back with another meeting, you will rush the closing, which is where you set expectations for reports and leave the team feeling valued.

## Opening the Workshop (First 10 Minutes)

The opening sets the tone for everything that follows. Get this right and the rest flows. Get it wrong and you spend the rest of the session fighting defensiveness.

### What to Say

Start by thanking the team for their time. Then explain three things clearly:

**What this is:** "This is a facilitated self-assessment. You are the experts on your own practices. My job is to guide the conversation, not to evaluate you. There are no right or wrong answers -- only honest ones."

**How it works:** "I will walk you through a set of questions for each zone we are assessing today. You will score each question individually and privately first. Then we will reveal the scores as a group and discuss what we see. The whole thing takes about 90 minutes."

**How results are used:** "Your scores will go into a team report that your team and your direct leadership will see. The management report that goes to organizational leadership will contain aggregate patterns across teams -- it will not contain your team's specific scores. This separation is non-negotiable and it is the reason I can ask you to be truly honest."

### What NOT to Say

Avoid anything that frames the workshop as evaluation, comparison, or judgment:

- "Let's see how you stack up." (Implies comparison.)
- "The goal is to get to Zone 2." (Implies their current state is inadequate.)
- "Other teams we've assessed..." (Triggers comparison anxiety.)
- "Management wants to understand..." (Frames the diagnostic as surveillance.)
- "Don't worry, there's no wrong answer." (Paradoxically increases worry. Instead, normalize honestly: "I have seen strong teams score low in areas where they are still building habits. That is valuable information, not a problem.")

The tone you want is: curious, safe, and grounded. You are genuinely interested in how this team works. You are not there to catch them at anything.

## Running the Self-Assessment (Middle 60 Minutes)

### Introducing Each Zone

Before the team scores a zone, give a brief (2-3 minute) overview of what the zone represents. Use plain language, not framework jargon. For Zone 1, you might say:

"Zone 1 is about individual AI tool usage becoming habitual. The key question is whether your team members reach for AI tools as a natural part of their workflow -- not just when things are calm, but also when deadlines are tight or when you are working in unfamiliar code. We are looking at what you actually do day-to-day, not what you know how to do or what you do on your best day."

Then read through the questions. For each question, give participants a moment to think, then ask them to record their score privately.

### Handling Individual Scoring

Individual scoring must happen before any group discussion. This is critical. If people discuss scores before recording them, anchoring bias takes over -- the first person to speak influences everyone else.

In person, have participants write scores on paper. Remotely, use anonymous polls or private message submission. Do not ask people to unmute and say their scores aloud. The goal is independent, honest individual assessment.

Give the team about 5-7 minutes per zone to score all questions. Watch the room (or the poll responses). When most people seem finished, give a 30-second warning before moving on.

### Revealing Scores and Reading the Distribution

Once everyone has scored, display the results. If you are using paper, tally scores on a flip chart or whiteboard. If digital, share your screen showing the aggregated results.

Read the distribution aloud. Do not just state averages. Describe the shape: "On question 3, we have a pretty tight cluster around 4, with one person at 2. That variance is interesting -- let's come back to that in discussion." Or: "Question 6 shows a clear split -- three people scored 4 or 5, and two people scored 1 or 2. That's a bimodal pattern, and it usually tells us something important about role differences or adoption gaps."

Averages are useful for determining [competency stages](/toolkit/scoring-thresholds), but distributions tell the story. A 3.5 average that comes from everyone scoring 3 or 4 is very different from a 3.5 average that comes from half the team scoring 5 and the other half scoring 2.

### What to Do with High-Variance Items

High-variance items -- where the team disagrees significantly -- are your most valuable facilitation opportunities. They reveal:

- **Role-based gaps.** Engineers score high on coding questions; PMs score low because AI has not been integrated into their workflow. This is a common Zone 1 pattern.
- **Adoption inconsistency.** Some team members have deeply adopted AI tools; others have not. This often reflects differences in training, tool access, or individual comfort with AI.
- **Pressure resilience gaps.** Some members maintain AI practices under pressure; others drop them. This is the difference between knowledge and [competency](/toolkit/competency-vs-knowledge).

When you see high variance, do not try to resolve it. Instead, name it: "We have a wide spread here. I'd like to hear from someone who scored high and someone who scored low. What does this look like in your day-to-day work?" Let the team explore the variance. The discussion itself builds shared understanding.

## Facilitating the Discussion (20-30 Minutes)

### Opening Productive Discussion

After the scores are revealed for all zones being assessed, shift into facilitated discussion. This is where the diagnostic becomes more than a survey.

Start with an open question: "Looking at these results, what stands out to you? What surprised you, and what confirmed what you already suspected?"

Let the team react first. Often, they will identify the most important patterns themselves. Your job is to listen, probe, and redirect when the conversation becomes unproductive.

### Listening for Organizational Blockers

As the team discusses their scores, listen for themes that point to organizational factors rather than individual behaviors. These are the insights that make the management report valuable.

**Individual behavior:** "I keep meaning to try AI for testing, but I just haven't gotten around to it." This is an adoption issue that the team can address.

**Organizational blocker:** "We wanted to integrate AI into our CI pipeline, but IT won't approve the tool." This is a structural barrier that requires organizational investment. Before recording a theme as a finding, share your interpretation with the team: "I am hearing that the IT approval process is a significant barrier to your AI tooling integration. Is that an accurate summary, or is there more to it?" This testing step validates your interpretation and often surfaces additional information that refines the finding. Note it carefully -- it will appear in the management report as a systemic finding.

Other organizational blockers to listen for: unclear policies on AI tool usage, procurement delays for licenses, security restrictions that have not been revisited, management that has not signaled support for AI adoption, and missing training or onboarding for AI tools.

### Moving from Problem to Investment Framing

When the team identifies a gap, gently shift the conversation from "this is a problem" to "this is an investment opportunity." The ACE framework is built on the concept that zone transitions require [organizational investments](/toolkit/organizational-investments), not just individual effort.

Instead of: "We're bad at this."
Guide toward: "What would need to change in our environment for this behavior to become habitual?"

Instead of: "Our PM doesn't use AI."
Guide toward: "What tools, training, or workflow changes would make AI useful for product management work on this team?"

This reframing is not just positive spin. It directly connects diagnostic findings to the roadmap work that follows in Phase 3. The team begins to see their scores not as a report card but as a starting point for investment planning.

## Closing Well (Last 10 Minutes)

The closing is more important than most facilitators realize. How you end determines what the team takes away.

### Summarize What You Heard

Briefly reflect back the key themes from the discussion. Keep it to 3-4 points: "Here is what I heard today. You have strong individual adoption of AI coding tools, especially among developers. There is a gap in PM and design adoption. The team has high variance on pressure resilience -- some of you maintain AI practices under deadline pressure, others revert. And there is a recurring theme about policy clarity blocking deeper integration."

### Set Expectations for Reports

Tell the team what happens next: "I will compile the scores and discussion themes into a team report. You and your team lead will receive it within [timeframe]. The management report will contain aggregate patterns across all teams we assess -- it will not include your specific scores or the details of our conversation today."

### Leave the Team Feeling Capable, Not Judged

Regardless of where the team scored, close with genuine acknowledgment: "Honest self-assessment takes courage. The fact that you were willing to look at your practices honestly puts you in a better position than teams that inflate their scores. These results give you a real foundation to work from."

Never close with: "You have some work to do." Instead: "You have a clear picture of where you are and what investments would move you forward."

## After the Workshop

### Writing the Team Report

Write the report within a few days while the workshop is fresh. Include: current zone and competency stage, score distributions (not just averages), key themes from the discussion, identified strengths, growth areas, and specific investment recommendations. See the report templates referenced in the [engagement model](/toolkit/engagement-model).

### Preparing the Management Report

The management report aggregates patterns across teams. It includes systemic findings -- organizational factors that affect multiple teams. It does not include individual team scores. If you find yourself tempted to include team-specific data because it illustrates a point, anonymize it or describe the pattern generically: "Several teams reported that unclear AI usage policies suppress adoption" rather than "Team X scored low because of policy confusion."

### What to Do with Your Own Notes

Your facilitation notes -- the things you observed, the body language you noticed, the tensions you sensed -- are valuable for your own development but do not belong in reports. Keep a private facilitation journal. After each workshop, note: what went well, what you would do differently, what surprised you, and what patterns you are seeing across workshops. This journal is how you build expertise over time. Review it before your next engagement.
